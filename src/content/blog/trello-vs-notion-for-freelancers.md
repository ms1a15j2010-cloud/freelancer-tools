Trello and Notion are two of the most popular free tools for organizing client work, and freelancers often wonder which one to use. They overlap, but they are built for different jobs. One keeps your tasks moving on a visual board. The other lets you build a whole workspace for your notes, projects and client details.

The short version: pick Trello if you want to track tasks quickly and get started in minutes. Pick Notion if you want one place for tasks, notes, client pages and documents, and you do not mind spending time setting it up. Free plans, limits and prices change often, so check each company's pricing page before you commit.

## At a glance

| | Trello | Notion |
|---|---|---|
| Free plan | Yes, with unlimited cards and a limit of 10 boards per workspace | Yes, with unlimited pages for individuals |
| Paid plans | Roughly $5 to $6 per user a month for the first paid tier | Roughly $10 to $12 per user a month for the first paid tier |
| Core idea | Visual boards with cards that move between columns | Flexible pages, notes and databases |
| Setup time | Minutes | Longer, since you build your own system |
| Best for | Simple task tracking and visual thinkers | One hub for tasks, notes and client information |

## Pricing

Both tools have a free plan that works well for one person. Trello's free plan includes unlimited cards and a limit of 10 boards per workspace, which can run out quickly if you keep a separate board for every client. Notion's free plan gives individuals unlimited pages, though collaboration features are more limited.

On paid plans, Trello is generally the cheaper of the two. Its first paid tier is reported at roughly $5 to $6 per user a month, while Notion's is reported at roughly $10 to $12. Add-ons can cost extra on both, so read what each plan includes.

## How they work

Trello is built around boards, lists and cards. You create a card for each task, then drag it from "To do" to "Doing" to "Done." There is almost nothing to configure, which is why most people understand it within minutes.

Notion is more like a set of building blocks. You can create task boards, notes, documents, databases and client pages, and link them together. That flexibility is powerful, but it also means you have to design your own system before it becomes useful.

## Which handles client work better?

- **A simple project with a few tasks:** Trello is faster to set up and easier to read at a glance.
- **Many clients with lots of notes, files and details:** Notion lets you keep each client's information, tasks and documents together.
- **Sharing progress with clients:** Both can share boards or pages, but check what each plan allows before you promise a client access.
- **Invoices and billing:** Neither is an invoicing tool. See our guide to the [best project management tools for freelancers](/blog/best-project-management-tools-for-freelancers/) and our [invoicing guide](/blog/best-invoicing-tools-for-freelancers/) for tools that cover more.

## Who should pick Trello

- You want to start tracking tasks today, with no setup.
- You think visually and like moving cards across a board.
- You prefer a lower price when you upgrade.

## Who should pick Notion

- You want one place for tasks, notes, documents and client information.
- You enjoy building your own system and tweaking it over time.
- You want your project tracker and your knowledge base in the same tool.

## Our recommendation

If you are new to freelancing, start with Trello. It is easy to learn, and you can have your first board running in a few minutes. If you find yourself wanting notes, client pages and documents in the same place, try Notion. Some freelancers use both: Trello for tasks and Notion for notes. That is fine, but if you find yourself keeping the same information in two places, pick one.

Both are free to try, so spend a week on each with a real project and keep the one you actually open every day.
