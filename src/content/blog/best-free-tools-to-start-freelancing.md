You do not need to spend money to start freelancing. Most of what a new freelancer needs, such as email, documents, invoices, scheduling and design, is available on free plans. Start with these, then pay for a tool only when a free limit actually costs you time or money.

Free plans change often, so check each tool's current limits before you rely on it. Here is a simple starter stack, grouped by job.

## The $0 starter stack at a glance

- **Email, documents and file delivery:** Google account (Gmail, Docs, Drive, Calendar, Meet)
- **Invoices and basic bookkeeping:** Wave or Zoho Invoice
- **Design and proposals:** Canva
- **Scheduling client calls:** Calendly
- **Tasks and projects:** Trello or Notion
- **Time tracking:** Toggl Track or Clockify
- **Tracking leads:** HubSpot CRM
- **Polishing your writing:** Grammarly

## Google account: email, documents, storage and calls

A free Google account gives you Gmail, Docs, Sheets, Drive, Calendar and Meet. Drive includes 15 GB of free storage, and Meet is free for personal accounts, though free group calls have time limits that Google can change. Clients are used to receiving Google Docs links and Drive folders, which makes this a safe default for delivering work. A custom business email address costs extra, but it is not needed on day one.

## Wave or Zoho Invoice: getting paid

Wave has a free plan that covers invoicing and basic accounting, and Zoho Invoice is free and adds recurring invoices and a client portal. Card payments through either carry processing fees. If you want to compare more options, see our full guide to invoicing tools.

## Canva: design and proposals

Canva's free plan includes thousands of templates and basic editing, which is enough for proposals, social posts and simple logos. Some premium templates and features need a paid plan.

## Calendly: booking calls

Calendly's free plan lets clients book one-to-one calls through a link, with limits such as a single event type. Put the link on your website and in your email signature so you avoid back-and-forth emails.

## Trello or Notion: staying organized

Trello offers free kanban boards for tracking work, and Notion has a free plan for notes, tasks and client pages. Pick one. Using both usually means you end up using neither well.

## Toggl Track or Clockify: tracking time

Both have free plans that cover basic time tracking. Free plan limits, such as how many users are included, have changed over time, so check them before you sign up. If you bill by the hour, start tracking from your first client.

## HubSpot CRM: remembering your leads

HubSpot's CRM has a free plan with a contact list and deal pipeline. Even a few leads are easier to follow up on when they are in one place. A spreadsheet works too, if you would rather start simpler.

## Grammarly: cleaner writing

Grammarly's free plan checks spelling, grammar and tone as you write. It helps most when you write to clients by email or deliver written work.

## What to pay for first

When you start earning, these are the upgrades that usually pay for themselves, roughly in this order:

1. **A custom domain and business email**, so you look established.
2. **A paid plan on the tool that wastes your time**, such as invoicing or scheduling, once you hit its limit.
3. **A portfolio site on a paid plan**, if you want your own domain without platform branding.

## How to start

1. Pick one skill and one type of client.
2. Set up the Google account, one invoicing tool and one scheduling link.
3. Build a simple portfolio with two or three samples.
4. Contact your first prospects.

Keep your software costs low until the income justifies more. Many successful freelancers run on just a handful of tools.
